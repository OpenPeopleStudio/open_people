Keep power here
Public evidence brief · horizon desk · Newfoundland & Labrador

Keep the power here. Use it on loads that live here.

Newfoundland and Labrador generates about 43 TWh of green electricity a year and sends most of it across the Québec border. The Churchill Falls / Gull Island DCIA, signed 17 August 2026, is a framework — not binding power-purchase agreements. The House endorsed that framework 21–18 on 17 September. Binding paper is still ahead. The industrial question is whether firm power this province keeps is used here: mines and Labrador industry first. Compute is not the opener — it lives on a separate page.

01 — The situation

One paragraph

Newfoundland and Labrador owns one of the cleanest large power systems in North America. Churchill Falls alone — 5,428 MW, roughly 34 TWh a year — has been sold under contracts that captured a small fraction of what firm, carbon-free power commands today. In May 2026 the Independent Churchill River Review Committee found the December 2024 Memorandum of Understanding not in the long-term public interest as written, citing among other flaws limited provincial rights to use the power in-province. On August 17, 2026, NL Hydro, Hydro-Québec, and CF(L)Co signed a Definitive Cooperation and Implementation Agreement (DCIA), announced with Ottawa in St. John's: more retained megawatts for NL in public framing, a higher reported export price path, Labrador-West transmission, and a Labrador wind study. It is a framework, not binding PPAs. The House endorsed it 21–18 on 17 September 2026, with no referendum. That vote does not create contracts. Binding definitive agreements are targeted around 31 December 2026; the DCIA instrument can run to 31 March 2027. Québec votes 5 October. The province's stated industrial use is still framed around critical minerals. Mining matters, and it is first. Compute is optionality: not confirmed as reserved megawatts, price, queue, or policy preference versus Hydro-Québec or mining.

Labrador is not an infinite battery. It is enough firm green power to serve mines, towns, and other industry that lives here. Industry over raw export. Compute, if written eligible at all, is leftover optionality — see /compute.

That is 97.4% renewable generation, hydro-dominated, dispatchable, and concentrated at a handful of interconnection points. For scale: 1,000 MW running continuously consumes about 8.76 TWh a year. Churchill Falls is on the order of four such loads in energy terms — a genuinely significant resource, and a finite one. This is an argument for allocating it deliberately, not for pretending it is limitless.

03 — The value gap

The export price is rising. The value-added still leaves the province.

Under the 1969-lineage contract structure, Churchill Falls base energy was priced on the order of 0.2¢/kWh as an export price. Media-reported figures put the cumulative split to 2019 at roughly $28 billion in profit to Hydro-Québec against $2 billion to Newfoundland and Labrador.

The rejected December 2024 MOU headline was an average of 5.9¢/kWh in 2024 dollars. The Independent Review's technical briefing decomposes that average into its real path: about 3.8¢/kWh from 2024 to 2041, then about 16.7¢/kWh from 2042 to 2075. That path is now historical. NL Hydro has said Hydro-Québec will pay 1.8¢/kWh beginning in 2027, escalating about 14% a year to 11.5¢/kWh by 2041. The Government of Newfoundland and Labrador values the new package at $49 billion NPV (2026) and $273 billion nominal — up from $36 billion / $225 billion under the 2024 MOU.

Meanwhile, in February 2026, Hydro-Québec proposed a dedicated data-centre tariff of 13¢/kWh — roughly double its large-industrial rate — and still expects takers. Québec is deliberately rationing and repricing exactly the hydro-seeking compute demand that Labrador could serve. Near-term, Newfoundland and Labrador would still be selling the underlying power at a fraction of that retail.

A better export contract is not the same as keeping the industry. The province can now choose to use its retained block at home, or sell it. That choice is the policy. The point is not blame. It is that the input–output spread is still a policy choice, and the architecture is being written now.
Language discipline

The historical 0.2¢ figure is an export price under a specific contract. It is not a rate available to any customer today, and we never present it as one. Commercial power on the island runs roughly 11–13¢/kWh delivered. This brief is a value-retention argument, not a grievance argument.

Wind SPE under DCIA §13 is unnamed; NL Hydro sole discretion; federal Canada (not CPP) up to 40% SPE equity. A ~$8B / 100% CPP “A” wind company remains unknown.

05 — Neighbouring grids (context)

Demand has repriced power elsewhere. That is not a Labrador reservation.

Neighbouring-grid facts that used to open this brief now live on the compute quarantine page. They are context for leftover firm hydro — not a campus pitch, and not a named industrial class in the DCIA Material Terms.

The International Energy Agency projects global data-centre electricity consumption to grow from 415 TWh in 2024 to about 945 TWh by 2030. Power availability — not latency, not tax incentives — has become the primary site-selection criterion in those markets. Interconnection queues in the established hubs now run for years. None of that allocates Labrador megawatts.

Every Canadian hydro province is rationing. Ours has no policy at all.

Jurisdiction2025–26 posture
Québec Ministerial approval required for large new loads; proposed dedicated data-centre tariff of 13¢/kWh (Feb 2026, before the regulator).
British Columbia Crypto loads barred; competitive AI power call capped in the range of 300 MW.
Ontario Economic screening introduced for large load connections.
Alberta An interim connection cap on the order of 1,200 MW against multi-gigawatt request queues.
Newfoundland & Labrador No policy. No tariff. No allocation process. No project.

"Nobody builds AI infrastructure somewhere like Labrador"

Remote, cold and hydro-rich is precisely the profile of the current build-out. Stargate Norway, announced August 2025, placed a 230 MW campus scaling to 520 MW and roughly 100,000 GPUs in Narvik — above the Arctic Circle — as a joint venture between Nscale and Aker, running on hydropower, chosen for surplus clean power and cold climate. Meta has operated a hydro-powered campus in Luleå, Sweden since 2013. Google is expanding in Hamina, Finland.

Two technical facts make this work. AI training is latency-insensitive — training runs tolerate around 100 ms between regions, because no end user is waiting. Latency-sensitive inference stays near cities; capital-intensive training goes to the power. And cold helps, but power decides: free cooling for eight to ten months a year is a real efficiency edge, but liquid cooling is narrowing it. The durable advantage is firm, clean, uncommitted power. On that measure Labrador's position is close to unique in eastern North America.

Ottawa is already paying for this

Canada's Sovereign AI Compute Strategy commits more than $2 billion, with further Budget 2025 funding, and explicitly seeks Canadian-owned, Canadian-controlled projects above 100 MW with Indigenous participation. A Labrador consortium — Crown power, Innu equity, an NL developer, Canadian anchor demand — scores on every one of those criteria. The first intake window closed in 2026 with no Newfoundland and Labrador entrant, because there was no NL project to enter.

06 — The ask

Five things, none of which cost money

The province is not being asked to spend, build, or subsidize. It is being asked to write five instruments into the architecture of the binding agreements — or the provincial framework that sits beside them — before those agreements lock. House endorsement is not that lock. Year-end 2026 is the public target; the DCIA can run to 31 March 2027.

01A named block

Our ask, not a reserved block. A scheduled allocation of Labrador capacity eligible for industrial load, including compute, drawn from the publicly framed ~2,350 MW retain from Churchill Falls and Gull Island (plus wind if built). No firm MW, price, or queue for Labrador compute is confirmed. An illustrative first tranche in the 100–150 MW range as new generation phases in is an Open People ask — not a government figure.

02A transparent tariff

A published firm industrial compute rate — indicatively in the 4–6¢/kWh range plus full network-addition cost causation. High enough to be unimpeachable after Muskrat Falls; low enough to beat Québec's proposed 13¢.

03Allocation criteria that keep the value home

Score for NL majority ownership, Indigenous equity participation, creditworthy offtake, local employment, and balance-sheet substance. These criteria — not a ban — are the durable answer to the Great North Data experience.

04An infrastructure audit

Direct NL Hydro to publish Labrador dark-fibre capacity and recall-block headroom, and commission a diverse-route fibre feasibility study — co-fundable with federal connectivity programs.

05A federal handshake

A joint NL–Canada expression of interest under the Sovereign AI Compute Strategy, so the next intake window opens with a Labrador consortium already on file.

Item 1 belongs in the binding agreements' architecture or their enabling provincial framework. Items 2 through 5 are ordinary policy instruments the province can move before those agreements lock.

The cost of doing nothing

  • The retained block defaults to mining and re-export. Public framing gives NL optionality on about 2,350 MW plus wind if built. If other industrial uses — including compute — are not named as eligible, that optionality will be spent on other files — lawfully, and without a second chance inside this architecture.
  • The federal build-out passes the province by. Every subsidized megawatt lands where a project was ready. The first window already closed without an NL entry.
  • Labrador's industrial story stays minerals-only. Mining is vital, but it is cyclical and it employs at the mine, not at the grid.
  • 2041 arrives with no domestic alternative. The province's leverage in any future negotiation is a credible in-province use for the power — mines, towns, other industry, and compute if written eligible. That leverage's absence is why 1969 happened.
07 — Who builds it

A consortium, not a champion

The structure that works — demonstrated at Narvik, consistent with the Independent Review's governance critique, and politically viable after Muskrat Falls — puts each risk with the party built to carry it.

Power & site
Crown utility — capacity allocation, brownfield land at the point of generation, transmission.
Indigenous equity
Innu Nation and its development entities — ownership from day one, not consultation at the end. The structure federal programs explicitly reward.
Development
An NL-based developer entity that assembles the coalition and carries the project.
Catalyst / demand
Where Open People would sit — if partners build. Public campaign for in-province use; optional software layer. Open People is not a DCIA party, offtake seat, or demand seat, and does not assemble bankable offtake today. This is a catalyst role, not a deal seat.
Software layer
Where Open People sits. The sovereignty gateway, guardrails, and model hosting that make in-province compute usable by buyers who cannot send data offshore.
Capital
Infrastructure investors and federal programs — following the power allocation, never preceding it.

Capital does not create power; power attracts capital. Québec, British Columbia, Ontario and Alberta have each just demonstrated which direction that queue runs.

08 — Honest constraints

What is genuinely hard about this

A case built on cheerleading will fail in this province, and it should. Five constraints are real. None of them is a reason to leave the block unwritten — because a block that is never reserved is a permanent decision in favour of exporting raw electrons.

Fibre is the weakest link

Labrador's terrestrial backbone is essentially a single route of roughly 48 fibres built in 2012. Region-wide outages from single events are on record, and a subsidized Labrador broadband build was abandoned in May 2025 after a large cost overrun. Hyperscale campuses want multiple physically diverse paths; Labrador has approximately one.

What it requiresAn audit of dark-fibre capacity on existing utility routes, a costed diverse-route study, and — the transformative option — anchoring a transatlantic cable landing. Training workloads tolerate thin connectivity in a way no other data-centre segment does. This gates hyperscale, not a first sovereign node.
The workforce is already spoken for

The Churchill expansion program will itself absorb the province's construction trades at camp premiums. Muskrat Falls remains the controlling cost precedent for remote Labrador construction.

What it requiresModular, factory-built construction — now standard for precisely this reason — sequencing into the post-peak window, and treating the first facility as deliberately small.
Nothing large proceeds without Innu consent — nor should it

Ratification of any Innu Nation partnership on Gull Island remains unresolved. Geotechnical work at Gull Island was halted by blockade in July 2025. By 17–18 September 2026 Innu Nation wrote MHAs urging them not to vote; reporting described the royalty / Gull Island tariff path as worse than the 2024 MOU. Premier Wakeham said he will meet. That is a start, not consent, and not a partnership structure.

What it requiresInverting the standard model. Equity partnership in the compute entity from day one — a structure federal programs reward, that Labrador's impact-benefit culture supports, and that converts the project's largest political risk into its strongest political asset.
The Great North Data precedent

The province's one prior data-centre experience was a Labrador crypto operator that went bankrupt in 2019 owing money to the utility and public agencies. Municipal skepticism toward container mining is earned.

What it requiresNaming it rather than dodging it. That operation failed on balance sheet, and it was crypto — a commodity workload with no customers, no jobs, and no sovereignty value. An allocation framework that scores creditworthiness, ownership, employment and Indigenous equity is a filter it would have failed on every line.
AI demand could correct

Lease cancellations in 2025 and a credible skeptic corpus warn of overbuild. If capex contracts later this decade, marginal sites die first.

What it requiresTwo design choices. Cost position — firm clean power in the 4–6¢ band survives a correction that strands 13¢ sites. And demand mix — sovereign, defence-adjacent and federally subsidized Canadian compute is less bubble-correlated than merchant hyperscale. A correction is an argument for the low-cost sovereign version of this project, not against it.

10 — Falsifiability

What we have not proven

This document is meant to be argued with. The following are open questions, not settled claims, and we say so in every room:

  • That the province will allocate firm Labrador power to compute over mining. Critical minerals are named first in current priorities; compute has to earn peer status on the merits. No firm MW, price, queue, or policy preference for Labrador compute is confirmed.
  • That fibre economics support a training cluster at hyperscale path diversity. A first node likely fits; hyperscale is unproven until the route studies exist.
  • That public-sector demand alone is large enough to bank a facility. It almost certainly needs commercial and federal co-funding alongside.
  • That binding definitive agreements will be signed on the announced timeline, or that they will free near-term megawatts for a compute node. House endorsement of the DCIA framework is not that signature.
  • The current Labrador industrial rate schedule. Published historical figures are a decade old; we are confirming current schedules directly rather than quoting stale ones.
  • The reconciliation between the government's 2,350 MW retained-capacity figure and other published splits of the existing Churchill Falls plant. Public releases also conflict on Churchill Falls upgrade megawatts and Gull Island plant size (2,250 MW vs 2,700 MW) — we do not pick a number.
  • That a three-year notice recall, as described to the House, appears in signed contract text.
  • That 2026 Gull Island matches the 2012 Lower Churchill federal review scope. IAAC has not received that proponent confirmation.
  • The name of the Wind SPE under DCIA §13. It is unnamed in public framing; federal Canada (not CPP) up to 40% SPE equity is described; a ~$8B / 100% CPP-held “A” company remains unknown.
